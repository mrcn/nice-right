# Brand Voice

## Who We're Writing For

**Primary audience:** Small and mid-size business owners making decisions about AI adoption. Not developers. Not tech-native. They have a ChatGPT subscription, vague anxiety about falling behind, and real decisions to make. They read to make better decisions, not to become experts.

**Secondary audience:** Practitioners — technically capable, often developers or ops leads — who value rigor and will notice when sources are lazy or claims are inflated. They can handle complexity. They appreciate when we're honest about uncertainty.

**Not our audience (for SMB articles):** Total beginners who need AI explained from scratch. We're not writing explainers. We're writing for people who already know what ChatGPT is and are past "should I care?" — they're at "what should I actually do?"

---

## Tone

**Direct.** Lead with the answer or the claim. Not the context, not the background, not the acknowledgment that this is a complex topic.

**Confident on claims we've earned.** We state what the evidence shows. We don't soften every sentence into mush. If the data points one direction, say so.

**Honest about uncertainty.** When a source is vendor-funded, say so. When a number is a ceiling not an average, say so. When we don't know something, say we don't know. This is what makes the confident claims credible.

**No performance.** No "in today's rapidly evolving landscape." No "as we navigate the complexities of." No "it goes without saying." Say the thing.

**Expertise signal through precision, not jargon.** We use technical frameworks (Wardley, absorptive capacity, Gartner hype cycle) when they do genuine analytical work, not to sound smart. When we introduce a framework, we define it in one sentence and move on.

## Analogies That Work for SMB Audiences

**Use:** Spreadsheets, email, CRM/Salesforce, hiring processes, job boards, accounting software
**Avoid:** Git, pull requests, APIs, CI/CD pipelines, "the stack," terminal commands

The test: would a restaurant owner or a regional manufacturer understand this analogy in under five seconds without Google?

## Source Standards

**Tier 1 (cite without caveat):** Government data (Census Bureau, BLS, Federal Reserve), peer-reviewed academic research with disclosed methodology, independent research organizations (OECD, Brookings, Pew)

**Tier 2 (cite with disclosure):** Vendor-funded surveys, consulting firm research (McKinsey, BCG, Gartner), trade associations. Flag these inline: "*(vendor-funded — treat as directional)*"

**Tier 3 (use with skepticism or cut):** Company blog posts, press releases, self-published surveys with no disclosed methodology or sample size. These can be cited for practitioner patterns and documented implementations, not for statistics.

**Red flags:** "Up to X%" (cite the ceiling, not the average), statistics with no primary source, "studies show" without naming the study.

**Citation linking (mandatory):** Every citation that has a publicly accessible source URL must be hyperlinked — not just named. Link to the primary source, not a summary or third-party reference. **Never infer or construct a URL — only use URLs that have been verified to exist.** If a URL hasn't been verified, mark it `[URL needed]` rather than guessing. Where only a domain is confirmed (e.g., `fedsmallbusiness.org`) but the exact page path isn't, link the domain. Guessed URL paths are worse than no link — they send readers to 404s and signal sloppiness. This applies to sources footers and inline citations equally.

## What Makes Our Articles Fail

These apply across all four types. Internalize them.

**Audience mismatch.** Writing for the reader you wish you had. The SMB owner doesn't know what Git is. The developer reader doesn't need three paragraphs of AI 101. Know who you're writing for per article and don't drift.

**Fear-as-urgency.** "Act before it's too late" is manipulation, not argument. If the claim is right, the urgency comes from the logic. If you have to manufacture it, the claim isn't strong enough.

**Jargon as credibility.** Using framework names (Wardley map, absorptive capacity, hype cycle) without doing the work of explaining what they show. The framework does the work. The name is just a label.

**Treating the article as a container for everything we know.** The article has one job. Everything that doesn't serve that job makes the job harder.

**Burying the claim.** Especially for argument pieces: the central claim should be statable in one sentence and visible in the first scroll. If you can't state it that way, the argument isn't sharp enough yet.

**False authority from uncertain data.** A vendor statistic cited without disclosure. A correlation stated as causation. A ceiling presented as a typical result. These are integrity failures. Readers who know the field notice. Readers who don't eventually encounter the conflicting evidence and feel misled.

**Conclusions that don't follow.** The ending should feel like the only place the argument could land. If the conclusion surprises, the structure is wrong, not the conclusion.

## Voice by Article Type

**Utilitarian:** Terse. Numbered where possible. No preamble. The checklist is the content.

**Exploratory:** Methodical. Shows the work. Names uncertainty explicitly. Has a point of view earned by the investigation.

**Narrative:** Specific. Names, dates, details. No corporate case study voice. Has a character facing a real decision with real stakes.

**Argument/Thesis:** Confident. States the claim plainly in the opening. Doesn't hedge the core claim. Acknowledges real limits specifically (not as a general disclaimer). Ends where the logic leads, not where it's comfortable.
